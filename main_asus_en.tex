% === ABSTRACT ===
\begin{abstract}
Asymmetric Multicore Processors (AMP) pair high-capacity Performance cores with lower-power Efficient cores, and Linux CFS with Energy Aware Scheduling (EAS) is the default policy for placing threads across such clusters. Whether an application should override that policy with explicit static core pinning is usually settled on idle benchmark machines, a condition under which deployed edge devices almost never operate. Using SyncPilot, an 8-stage pipeline-parallel implementation of FSRCNN video super-resolution, we compare static Performance/Efficient pinning against unpinned CFS/EAS on an ASUS GX10 (10$\times$Cortex-X925 + 10$\times$Cortex-A725) across seven load conditions: idle, 4 and 10 background CPU-bound threads, and 2--5 concurrently running FSRCNN applications. On an idle system CFS/EAS wins by 8.2\% (1050 vs.\ 1136~ms). The ordering inverts as soon as the machine is not idle: static pinning leads by 3.1--19.8\% at every load condition tested, all at $p<10^{-4}$ (Welch, $n=7$), and is markedly steadier, with run-to-run standard deviation 13.0 vs.\ 46.7~ms under heavy noise and worst-case run latency growing 23.1\% from idle against 71.4\%. We attribute the inversion to the kernel's documented disabling of EAS once any CPU is overutilized, after which the fallback balancer may migrate critical-path stages onto Efficient cores. One ablation qualifies the result: adding cost-based stage routing on top of pinning costs 3.6--8.3\%. A 60-run calibration sweep explains why. The per-stage cost model is near-deterministic in isolation (1\% spread) but the table the framework builds \emph{in situ} degrades monotonically with concurrency to a 2.52$\times$ spread at twenty workers, and its threshold partitions load without regard to the clusters' measured 1.83--1.90$\times$ capacity ratio. The benefit comes from topology-aware pinning alone, which needs no runtime measurement.
\end{abstract}

% === I. INTRODUCTION ===
\section{Introduction}
The rapid evolution of edge computing has brought computationally intensive tasks, such as Deep Neural Network (DNN) inference, from cloud servers to local devices \cite{shi2016edge, zhou2019edge}. Modern edge hardware increasingly adopts Asymmetric Multicore Processors (AMP), notably the ARM big.LITTLE architecture, to balance high performance with energy efficiency \cite{greenhalgh2011biglittle, arm2013biglittle}. This architecture combines high-capacity ``Performance'' cores with lower-power ``Efficient'' cores.

Placing application threads across such clusters is, by default, the operating system's job. On Linux, the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS) augmented with Energy Aware Scheduling (EAS) estimates per-task utilization and places tasks on the cluster that minimizes predicted energy while meeting capacity. An application that believes it knows better can override this with explicit CPU affinity \cite{man7pthreadaffinity}, hard-pinning each worker thread to a core of a known class. The literature supports both positions. Capacity-aware OS schedulers were introduced because generic CFS treats cores as interchangeable \cite{koufaty2010bias, vancraeynest2012pie, saez2010comprehensive}; static partitioning approaches hold that offline knowledge of the workload beats what the OS can infer at runtime \cite{wang2020pipeit, wang2021asymo}.

This question has usually been asked under conditions that predetermine the answer. Scheduler comparisons are conventionally run on quiesced benchmark machines, where the OS faces no competing demand and has the whole span of a run to converge on a good placement. Deployed edge devices are not quiesced. They service network I/O, UI rendering, background daemons, and often several inference tenants at once. Idle-system throughput therefore cannot settle whether static pinning is worth its rigidity.

We therefore measure the same application across a contention sweep instead of at a single operating point. Our vehicle is SyncPilot, a pull-based pipeline framework in which FSRCNN super-resolution \cite{dong2016fsrcnn} is split into 8 stages served by a shared worker pool. One binary runs under three scheduling policies (static Performance/Efficient pinning, static pinning plus cost-based stage routing, and unpinned CFS/EAS) at seven contention levels on an ASUS GX10. The result is a crossover: CFS/EAS is the better policy on an idle machine and the worse policy at every load we measured.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We evaluate static core pinning against Linux CFS/EAS across a seven-point contention sweep covering two noise families, synthetic CPU-bound threads and full co-running FSRCNN tenants, instead of the single idle operating point typical of prior AMP scheduling evaluations.
\item We report a crossover result with its mechanism: unpinned CFS/EAS wins by 8.2\% on an idle system but loses by 3.1--19.8\% under every load condition tested, and degrades three times less predictably (run-to-run standard deviation 46.7 vs.\ 13.0~ms under heavy noise). We attribute the inversion to the kernel's documented disabling of EAS on overutilization \cite{linuxeas}, after which the fairness-driven fallback balancer is free to migrate critical-path stages onto Efficient cores.
\item We report a negative ablation. Layering cost-based stage routing, derived from a one-time per-stage cost calibration, on top of pinning degrades latency by 3.6--8.3\% at every contention level. A dedicated 60-run sweep of the calibration mechanism shows why. The per-stage cost model is near-deterministic when measured in isolation, but the table the framework builds while the pipeline runs degrades monotonically with concurrency, reaching a 2.52$\times$ run-to-run spread at twenty workers and flipping the heavy/light partition in 4 of 60 runs. Its threshold also partitions load without reference to hardware capacity. The advantage belongs to topology-aware static placement, which needs no runtime measurement at all.
\item We verify that 27 of the 28 distinct configurations reported here, including every contended one, produce bit-exact output against a serial ground truth; the 28th, a deliberately unsynchronized OpenMP control, corrupts 40.1\% of output bytes.
\end{itemize}

% === II. RELATED WORK ===
\section{Related Work}

Research on scheduling for AMP has evolved from static partitioning to dynamic load balancing. The work closest to ours is Annisa et al. \cite{annisa2025fsrcnn}, which we build on directly: it investigated the transition from serial to parallel FSRCNN execution on an Orange Pi 5 using OpenMP with explicit CPU affinity and a fixed thread-per-stage mapping. Their analysis showed that the heterogeneous big.LITTLE topology on that platform benefits from broader worker scaling, with the strongest gains occurring when the workload is distributed across both clusters instead of restricted to the fastest cores only, because the stronger LITTLE cores reduce the severity of the straggler effect. Their analysis also confirmed that layer 8 (deconvolution) remains the primary computational bottleneck across all configurations, exhibiting the lowest speedup due to memory dependencies and race conditions when multiple threads access adjacent pixel regions without proper synchronization. Their evaluation, like most in this line, was conducted on an otherwise idle board.

Wang et al. \cite{wang2020pipeit} proposed Pipe-it, a static partitioning method for CNN inference that relies on offline profiling to map layers to cores. AsyMo \cite{wang2021asymo} introduced offline planning for mobile CPUs but requires prior knowledge of the model structure. Recent works like DynPipe \cite{yuan2025dynpipe} utilize machine learning for dynamic adjustment but incur computational overhead during inference. Chronaki et al. \cite{chronaki2017task} propose dynamic and static scheduling policies for task-parallel programs on asymmetric multi-core systems based on critical-path detection and locality-aware task assignment; unlike the present work, their approach targets general task-dependency graphs, not the fixed, stage-structured pipeline mapping problem addressed here. Advanced frameworks such as QLSA-MOEAD \cite{saad2026qlsa} utilize reinforcement learning for heterogeneous scheduling but are designed for cloud-scale DAG tasks, making them too heavy for embedded pipeline processing.

On the OS side, bias scheduling \cite{koufaty2010bias} and PIE \cite{vancraeynest2012pie} established that asymmetry-blind fair scheduling wastes AMP capacity, and comprehensive AMP schedulers \cite{saez2010comprehensive} showed that the kernel can recover much of the loss without application cooperation. Linux's EAS is the production descendant of that line. Its documentation, however, states an explicit escape hatch: once any CPU in the root domain is overutilized, EAS is turned off and the generic load balancer resumes control \cite{linuxeas}. This documented behaviour is the natural organizing variable for an application-versus-OS placement comparison, yet AMP scheduling evaluations are conventionally reported at a single idle operating point, where the tipping point is never crossed and the fallback path never exercised. Structuring the evaluation around it, as we do here, is the methodological change this paper argues for; we make no claim that no prior work has observed the mechanism itself.

Our position relative to this body of work is therefore narrower than an ``improved scheduler'' claim. We do not propose a new scheduling algorithm; static thread-to-core affinity is a decades-old mechanism. What we contribute is the measurement that decides between it and the production OS policy for pipeline-parallel DNN inference, taken across contention levels instead of at the idle point, together with an ablation showing that the naive next step, feeding measured per-stage costs back into task routing, makes things worse on this platform.

% === III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE ===
\section{System Architecture}
SyncPilot is the vehicle for the comparison, not the object of the claim, so we describe it only to the depth needed to make the three compared policies precise. The framework consists of a Pipeline Engine, a Static Asymmetry-Aware Core Mapper (the policy under test), and an optional Cost-Gated Routing module (the ablated policy).

\subsection{Pipeline Engine}
SyncPilot adopts a centralized pull-based worker pool model. Unlike traditional pipeline architectures that assign a dedicated thread to each stage (thread-per-stage), our model decouples threads from stages.
The system consists of three primary components: (1) shared stage queues that hold pending tasks for each pipeline stage, (2) a worker pool comprising threads pinned to specific core types, and (3) a reorder buffer to ensure output order preservation.
A key advantage of this model is the elimination of a central dispatcher. Instead of a manager pushing tasks to workers, each worker thread autonomously pulls tasks from the shared queues. On every iteration, an idle worker scans the stage queues from the last stage toward the first and takes the first non-empty queue it can lock (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}), so that tasks closest to completion are drained preferentially, keeping the reorder buffer flowing and bounding in-flight memory. This decision is made locally by each worker thread via per-stage \texttt{trylock} operations instead of a global lock, avoiding the contention of a single central dispatcher.

\subsection{Static Asymmetry-Aware Core Mapping}
\label{sec:asymmap}
This is the policy the paper evaluates. Upon initialization, and before any task is processed, each worker thread is pinned to a specific CPU core via \texttt{pthread\_setaffinity\_np} \cite{man7pthreadaffinity}. SyncPilot detects Performance and Efficient cores automatically from \texttt{cpufreq/cpuinfo\_max\_freq} (the highest-frequency cluster is treated as Performance, the remainder as Efficient), or accepts an explicit core-ID list; workers are then assigned round-robin within each class, so that a 20-worker run on a 10+10 topology pins the first 10 workers to Performance cores and the remaining 10 to Efficient cores. This mapping is fixed for the lifetime of the run: it does not change in response to which stage a worker happens to be executing, and it consults no runtime measurement whatsoever.

Its role is purely topological: it gives every worker a dedicated core of known capability. The actual work-to-core matching emerges indirectly, since Performance-core workers execute the same priority-ordered pull loop faster than Efficient-core workers and therefore drain more tasks from the shared queues per unit time. Every worker, Performance or Efficient, applies the same priority rule; the only reason Performance-core workers end up handling a disproportionate share of the heaviest stage is that they execute it faster, not because they preferentially select it. Setting the environment variable \texttt{SYNCPILOT\_DISABLE\_AFFINITY=1} skips the pinning step entirely and yields the CFS/EAS baseline used throughout Section~\ref{sec:eval}: the identical binary, the identical worker count, and the identical pull loop, with thread placement left to the kernel.

\subsection{Cost-Gated Routing (Ablated Variant)}
\label{sec:capacityaware}
The framework also implements a second, richer policy that we evaluate as an ablation and ultimately recommend against on this platform. During the processing of the first task (Task ID 0), it measures the execution time of each stage using \texttt{clock\_gettime} and stores the result in a per-stage cost table; after that first task the measurement stops, so steady-state dispatch is a constant-time table lookup, not a timing call. A stage is then classified ``heavy'' once its measured cost exceeds $0.75\times$ the mean per-stage cost. Performance-core workers skip non-heavy stages in their normal search pass, and Efficient-core workers skip heavy stages unless no lighter task is available. That fallback is work-conserving and fires only when a worker would otherwise idle.

This gating is enabled by default in the FSRCNN example and is disabled by \texttt{SYNCPILOT\_DISABLE\_TWOPOOL=1}, which leaves pinning active and cost-based routing off. Two implementation details of this policy were forced by failures, not chosen. First, the fallback pass must be work-conserving for both classes. Our initial version enforced the preference strictly in both directions and let only Efficient workers relax it; on a homogeneous configuration (8 workers, all Performance) this deadlocks outright: every stage classified light is one no worker will accept, so the pipeline stalls instead of merely running slowly. Second, the framework deactivates gating automatically whenever all workers belong to a single core class, which is both a correctness guard against that failure and a recognition that the heavy/light split is meaningless without both clusters present; consequently the 4-, 8-, and 10-worker configurations reported in Section~\ref{sec:idle} are pure static-pinning runs regardless of the flag. The general lesson is that class-preference routing is not merely a performance heuristic that degrades gracefully: without a fully work-conserving escape it is a liveness hazard. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} reports the measured effect of this policy, which is negative at every contention level.

% === IV. IMPLEMENTATION ===
\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
The framework is implemented in C using POSIX Threads (pthreads) and compiled with GCC for the AArch64 architecture. We selected FSRCNN as the case study due to its heterogeneous layer characteristics: early layers are lightweight convolutions, while the final deconvolution layer is compute-intensive. The application is partitioned into 8 pipeline stages, one per FSRCNN layer.

\subsection{Queueing and Synchronization}
Each of the 8 stages owns an independent, fixed-capacity ring-buffer queue guarded by its own \texttt{pthread\_mutex\_t}; there is no engine-wide lock on the task-dispatch path. A worker acquires a task by scanning stages from last to first and attempting a non-blocking \texttt{pthread\_mutex\_trylock} on each stage's queue in turn (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pull}); if a lock is contended, the worker moves on to the next candidate stage instead of blocking, which is what we mean by ``without a global lock''. The queues themselves are still mutex-protected, not lock-free in the strict, non-blocking-algorithm sense. Backpressure is enforced through a per-stage \texttt{reserved\_slots} counter: a worker may only pop a task from stage $i$ if it can simultaneously reserve a free slot in stage $i{+}1$'s queue, preventing a fast upstream worker from overflowing a slower downstream stage.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Per-worker task pull (non-blocking, priority-ordered)}
\label{alg:pull}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \textbf{function} PullTask(core\_class):
\FOR{$stage \gets N{-}1$ \textbf{downto} $0$}
    \IF{$\text{trylock}(Q[stage])$ fails}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$Q[stage]$ is empty \textbf{or} next-stage slot unavailable}
        \STATE unlock; \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $task \gets \text{pop}(Q[stage])$; reserve next-stage slot; unlock
    \STATE \textbf{return} $task$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{None} \COMMENT{sleep on \texttt{cond\_work} until signaled}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Once a worker finishes executing a stage's callback, it pushes the task to stage $i{+}1$'s queue (or, if $i$ was the last stage, into the Reorder Buffer) and signals waiting workers. The Reorder Buffer is a pre-allocated array of \texttt{total\_tasks} slots indexed by task ID; a dedicated consumer thread blocks on a condition variable until slot \texttt{next\_expected\_id} is filled, invokes the user-supplied writer callback on it, clears the slot, and advances, which is what guarantees strictly ordered output despite workers completing tasks out of order.

% === V. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION ===
\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
We evaluated SyncPilot on an ASUS GX10 board. The GX10 is an NVIDIA GB10-based ARM workstation, not a classic mobile AMP SoC: its CPU cluster combines 10$\times$ Cortex-X925 (Performance cores) @ up to 3.9 GHz and 10$\times$ Cortex-A725 (Efficient cores) @ up to 2.808 GHz on the ARMv9-A DynamIQ hierarchy, with no ultra-low-power tier present (e.g., no Cortex-A5xx/A510). The two classes are \emph{interleaved}, not contiguous, in the kernel's CPU numbering: reading \texttt{cpufreq/cpuinfo\_max\_freq} on every core gives Performance cores at \texttt{cpu5--9} and \texttt{cpu15--19}, and Efficient cores at \texttt{cpu0--4} and \texttt{cpu10--14}. We state this explicitly because the obvious assumption of a contiguous \texttt{cpu0--9} / \texttt{cpu10--19} split is wrong on this board. Any experiment that pins by CPU index without checking would silently measure a mixture of both classes instead of either one. We use ``Performance core'' / ``Efficient core'' throughout this paper as vendor-neutral labels for the higher- and lower-capacity cluster, respectively, in preference to ARM's ``big.LITTLE'' naming, since Cortex-A725 is itself a high-throughput core and calling it ``LITTLE'' would overstate how power-constrained it actually is relative to a true ultra-efficiency core. Readers should not assume our scaling results transfer to platforms with a true ultra-low-power tier (e.g., Cortex-A55), where the straggler effect reported by \cite{annisa2025fsrcnn} on Orange Pi 5 is more severe; Section~\ref{sec:whenwrong} discusses how the narrow cluster gap on this platform bounds the generality of the crossover we report. The operating system was Ubuntu 24.04 with Linux Kernel 6.17.0-1018-nvidia, on which EAS is active for the unpinned baseline. Compilation was performed using GCC 13.3.0 with \texttt{-O3} optimization flags. We discuss the choice of a GPU-equipped workstation for a CPU-scheduling study in Section~\ref{sec:whycpu}.

As a case study, we deployed the Fast Super-Resolution Convolutional Neural Network (FSRCNN) \cite{dong2016fsrcnn} for low-to-high resolution video reconstruction (176$\times$144 $\rightarrow$ 352$\times$288 pixels, 150 frames of the Suzie sequence). FSRCNN is an ideal candidate due to its heterogeneous layer complexity. The pipeline was partitioned into 8 stages (Layer 1 to Layer 8).

All three policies run the identical \texttt{fsrcnn\_syncpilot} binary with 20 workers, differing only in environment flags:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Static pinning}: workers hard-pinned 10 to the Performance cluster and 10 to the Efficient cluster (Section~\ref{sec:asymmap}), cost-gated routing disabled.
\item \textbf{Static pinning + cost-gating}: the same pinning plus the cost-threshold stage routing of Section~\ref{sec:capacityaware}.
\item \textbf{CFS + EAS}: affinity disabled, 20 unpinned threads placed entirely by the kernel.
\end{itemize}

Each policy was measured at seven load conditions. The idle condition runs the benchmark alone on a quiesced board. Two \emph{synthetic noise} conditions add 4 or 10 unpinned CPU-bound OpenMP spin-loop threads (\texttt{noise\_generator}) alongside the measured application. Four \emph{multi-tenant} conditions instead run 2, 3, 4, or 5 additional complete FSRCNN applications concurrently; each tenant is the same binary configured with 4 workers and \texttt{SYNCPILOT\_DISABLE\_AFFINITY=1}, so neighbours are ordinary CFS-scheduled processes that do not themselves claim Performance cores, and each is restarted in a loop for the duration of the measured run to keep the background load continuous. Tenant output is redirected to \texttt{/dev/null} so the experiment isolates CPU and cache contention, not storage bandwidth. The multi-tenant family adds 8--20 background threads, comparable in thread count to the synthetic family, but with the memory footprint, cache access pattern, and multithreaded structure of a real neighbouring inference workload, not a pure ALU burner.

Every scenario was executed 8 times. Run 1 of each scenario is treated as a cold-start/profiling run and excluded; the reported average, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation are computed over the remaining 7 steady-state runs. Run 1 typically differs from steady state by cold page cache (weight files, input video read twice) and thread/engine initialization. All results in Tables~\ref{tab:idle}--\ref{tab:contention} come from a single benchmark session, so cross-scenario comparisons are not confounded by session-to-session drift. Significance is assessed with Welch's unequal-variance $t$-test over the 7 steady-state runs per condition. Output correctness was verified by comparing every one of the 150 output frames in each scenario against the corresponding ground-truth frame using byte-wise comparison and PSNR calculation.

The \texttt{cpufreq} governor was \texttt{performance} on all 20 cores, verified per core and not assumed. The evaluation does not control for SoC thermal state or isolate the GPU from concurrent activity, and no artifact/code release accompanies this submission at this time. We list these as remaining reproducibility gaps, not as controlled variables. The environment capture, per-core frequency and governor readout, and and the supporting measurements of Sections~\ref{sec:layerprofile} and \ref{sec:perfcounter} come from a single scripted collection run whose full log we retain.

\subsection{Why CPU Scheduling on a GPU-Equipped Workstation?}
\label{sec:whycpu}
The GX10's raison d'\^{e}tre is its integrated Blackwell-class GPU, and a reviewer might reasonably ask why we schedule DNN inference across its CPU cores at all. We use the GX10 not as a representative deployed edge device but as a convenient, currently-available proxy for the CPU-side topology that is becoming common on recent Armv9 heterogeneous designs (a high-capacity cluster paired with a second, still-substantial-throughput cluster, without a true ultra-efficiency tier). Such a topology is increasingly common even on GPU-equipped SoCs, where the CPU cluster still executes pre-/post-processing, control logic, or DNN operators that are not GPU-resident. Our results characterize scheduling behaviour on this CPU topology; they are not a claim that CPU-only inference is preferable to GPU inference on this hardware, and we make no throughput or efficiency comparison against the GX10's GPU in this paper.

\subsection{The Idle Operating Point Favours CFS/EAS}
\label{sec:cfsidle}
The last two rows of Table~\ref{tab:idle} are the ones that motivate the rest of the paper. On an idle system, unpinned CFS/EAS at 20 threads completes in 1050~ms against static pinning's 1136~ms. The OS policy is 8.2\% faster, and the gap is statistically solid ($t=9.8$, $p<10^{-5}$). At 10 workers the two policies are indistinguishable: 1342 vs.\ 1345~ms, a 3~ms difference against standard deviations of 17.5 and 6.8~ms ($t=0.4$, $p=0.68$).

The explanation is not that hard-pinning is intrinsically inferior but that an idle machine is the best case for a learning scheduler. Over a 150-frame run with no competing demand, EAS has both the utilization signal and the free capacity it needs to migrate compute-heavy threads toward Performance cores and light threads toward Efficient cores, converging on a mapping close to the one static pinning imposes, while keeping the freedom to use whichever core is momentarily idle and paying nothing for the rigidity of a fixed assignment. Reported alone, this measurement would recommend against static pinning. Section~\ref{sec:contention} shows why it should not be reported alone.

Table~\ref{tab:contention} and Figure~\ref{fig:contention} give the paper's central result. The idle-system ordering inverts as soon as the machine has anything else to do, and it stays inverted for every load condition we tested.

Under synthetic CPU noise the reversal is immediate. With 4 background spin-loop threads, static pinning is already ahead: 1225 vs.\ 1264~ms, a 3.1\% reduction ($t=5.7$, $p<10^{-4}$). At 10 background threads, enough to saturate the Performance cluster, the gap widens to 19.8\% (1401 vs.\ 1747~ms, $t=18.9$, $p<10^{-6}$). Crucially, the two policies degrade differently, not by a common factor: relative to its own idle baseline, static pinning grows 23.3\% while CFS/EAS grows 66.4\%.

The multi-tenant family is the more realistic of the two, since each neighbour is a full inference application with its own weight footprint and cache behaviour, not a pure ALU loop. Static pinning leads at every level: 10.1\% at 2 tenants, 10.5\% at 3, 12.0\% at 4, and 8.2\% at 5 ($p<10^{-4}$ throughout). The margin peaks at 4 tenants and narrows slightly at 5, which is consistent with saturation: at 5 tenants roughly 20 extra runnable threads compete for 20 cores, and no placement policy recovers capacity that no longer exists.

The standard-deviation columns show that the difference is not only a shift in mean latency. Under heavy synthetic noise, static pinning's run-to-run standard deviation is 13.0~ms against CFS/EAS's 46.7~ms, a 3.6$\times$ difference in dispersion, and its worst observed run is 1427~ms against 1836~ms. Measured from each policy's own idle worst case, static pinning's worst-case run latency grows 23.1\% (1159~ms to 1427~ms) while CFS/EAS's grows 71.4\% (1071~ms to 1836~ms). Under multi-tenant load the pattern holds but is less stark: at 4 tenants the standard deviations are 46.4 vs.\ 96.2~ms. For a latency-sensitive streaming deployment, a policy that is 8\% slower in the best case but 3--4$\times$ more predictable in the common case is the safer engineering choice.

One qualification applies to the ``worst-case'' framing: our instrumentation records one wall-clock time per full 150-frame run, not a timestamp per frame. The ``Worst'' column is therefore the slowest of 7 runs, not a frame-level tail latency. A genuine jitter characterization (per-frame latency distribution, p95/p99) would require finer-grained logging than we collected and is left as follow-up work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The standard-deviation columns are on firmer ground, being computed from the same 7 samples that produce the means.

\subsection{Ablation: Cost-Gated Stage Routing}
\label{sec:ablation}
Having established that explicit placement beats OS placement under load, the next question is whether a smarter explicit policy does better still: whether feeding measured per-stage costs back into task routing (Section~\ref{sec:capacityaware}) improves on topology-only pinning. The middle group of Table~\ref{tab:contention} answers this, and the answer is no.

Percentage gaps in this section are expressed relative to the faster policy: ``6.9\%'' means gating takes 6.9\% longer than plain pinning at that contention level. (Stated the other way round, relative to gating, the same idle gap reads 6.4\%.) On that basis, cost-gated routing is slower than plain static pinning at every one of the seven contention levels, by 6.9\% idle, 4.7\% and 7.3\% under synthetic noise, and 7.3\%, 3.6\%, 8.3\%, and 7.6\% across the multi-tenant levels. Six of the seven gaps are significant at $p<0.01$; the exception is the 3-tenant point ($p=0.016$), which remains significant at $p<0.05$. The mean penalty is roughly 6.5\%, and there is no contention level at which gating recovers.

The loss is not a loss of work conservation. Our implementation's fallback pass is fully work-conserving for both core classes: a worker that finds nothing acceptable in its preference-respecting scan repeats the scan in steal mode, where the preference test returns true unconditionally. A Performance worker therefore does take a light task when only light tasks are queued; it does not idle. Two other mechanisms account for the penalty. The first is failed-scan overhead: every gating rejection costs a full extra traversal. A worker scans all 8 stages, taking and releasing a \texttt{trylock} plus the calibration lock at each rejected stage, before it can fall through to the steal pass and scan all 8 again. This is pure overhead on the dispatch path and is paid whenever the queued work does not match the worker's class.

The second mechanism is the substantial one. The $0.75\times$ mean threshold derives the heavy/light load split entirely from the cost distribution across stages, and takes no account of hardware capacity. Across the 60-run calibration sweep of Section~\ref{sec:layerprofile} it reserves 73.8\% of measured per-frame cost for the 10 Performance cores at twenty workers (78.2\% on the isolated profile), leaving the 10 Efficient cores the remainder. At the measured capacity ratio a proportional allocation would be 65.5:34.5, so gating oversubscribes the Performance cluster by 8--13 percentage points while the Efficient cluster runs short of work; only the steal fallback, reached after the wasted scan, pulls the balance back. The emergent mapping under plain pinning has no such problem: Performance workers end up with a larger share of the work precisely because they finish tasks faster, so the load proportion self-adjusts to the true capacity ratio instead of being fixed in advance by a threshold that never sees it.

This also explains why the penalty is roughly constant in relative terms across the sweep. Both mechanisms are structural properties of the dispatch path and the partition ratio, not functions of how loaded the machine is, so they scale with total work, not with contention.

This is a negative result about the added policy, not about the workload model behind it: Section~\ref{sec:layerprofile} shows the underlying cost distribution is highly reproducible when measured in isolation. What fails is measuring it in situ and routing on the result. We report this because the cheaper policy winning is the actionable finding, and because an earlier version of this work assumed the opposite.

Measured in isolation, the cost model is excellent. At one worker the table is essentially deterministic: total cost varies by 1\% across ten runs, and per-stage standard deviations run from 0.2\% to 3.5\% of their means, below 1\% for six of the eight stages. It gives Layer~8 (deconvolution, $9\times9$) 54.8\% of per-frame cost, Layer~7 (expanding) 12.3\% and Layer~2 (shrinking) 11.2\%, with Layers 3--6 between 3.3\% and 4.1\%. What the framework actually calibrates, however, is not the isolated profile. Task~0 traverses the pipeline while up to nineteen other frames are already in flight, so every measurement absorbs whatever contention happens to be present. Table~\ref{tab:layers} and Figure~\ref{fig:costvar} show the consequence: the run-to-run spread in total measured cost grows monotonically with worker count, from 1.01$\times$ at one worker to 2.52$\times$ at twenty. At twenty workers, Layer~2's measured cost varies 4.1$\times$ between identical runs, Layer~5 3.9$\times$, Layer~6 3.3$\times$ and Layer~7 2.9$\times$.

In 4 of the 60 runs the threshold selected a different set of stages than the usual Layers 2, 7 and 8: twice at ten workers and once at twenty, Layer~3 crossed into the heavy set; once at eight workers, Layer~7 fell out of it. Figure~\ref{fig:costvar}(a) shows why these two stages and no others. At twenty workers Layer~7's range spans the threshold and Layer~3's upper tail reaches it, while every other stage stays clear on one side. The rate is modest: the classification is stable in roughly 93\% of runs, so this is tail behaviour, not the common case. But a scheduling policy that silently reassigns a stage to the wrong core class in one run out of fifteen is a policy whose behaviour cannot be reasoned about from its inputs.

A cost model can be, as here, both qualitatively correct and quantitatively reproducible in isolation, and still be the wrong thing to route on once it is measured in the environment it is meant to govern.

\subsection{Output Correctness Verification}
\label{sec:correctness}
Output correctness was verified across all 28 distinct configurations reported in this paper.\footnote{Count: Table~\ref{tab:idle}'s ten rows (two serial baselines; static pinning at 4, 8, 10 and 20 workers; cost-gated 20W; unpinned CFS/EAS at 10 and 20 workers; the naive OpenMP control), plus Table~\ref{tab:contention}'s 21 cells less its three idle cells, which are the same runs as Table~\ref{tab:idle}'s 20W static, 20W cost-gated and CFS-20W rows: $10 + (21 - 3) = 28$.} For the 27 SyncPilot and CFS/EAS configurations, covering every policy at every contention level plus the scaling configurations of Table~\ref{tab:idle}, every one of the 150 output frames was bit-exact against the serial ground truth, confirmed by byte-wise comparison (0 differing bytes) and infinite PSNR. This holds under contention as well as on an idle system, so the latency advantage reported in Section~\ref{sec:contention} does not come at a correctness cost, and neither does the CFS/EAS baseline lose correctness under load: reordering pressure is absorbed by the reorder buffer regardless of where the kernel places threads.

The contrast is with unsynchronized parallelization. Rather than relying on \cite{annisa2025fsrcnn}'s report that parallel FSRCNN on Efficient CPUs produced visible artifacts, we reproduced the failure mode on our own platform: a naively-parallelized OpenMP baseline (\texttt{fsrcnn\_naive\_openmp}, 20 threads, no reorder buffer or stage-level synchronization) differed from the ground truth in 9{,}155{,}530 of 22{,}809{,}600 output bytes (40.1\%), at an average PSNR of 24.08~dB. It was also 5.5$\times$ slower than static-pinned SyncPilot at the same thread count (6215 vs.\ 1136~ms), a timing we give as context only, since a run that computes the wrong answer is not a meaningful performance baseline. Those figures are from the same session as Tables~\ref{tab:idle}--\ref{tab:contention}; the corruption is itself nondeterministic, and two earlier sessions on the same binary and input gave 43.7\% / 22.63~dB and 46.9\% / 22.37~dB. The instability is the point: an unsynchronized parallelization does not fail reproducibly, which is what makes it hard to catch in testing. SyncPilot's pull-based worker pool with per-stage queues and a reorder buffer rules out that class of race condition by construction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Ordering Inverts: EAS Under Overutilization}
\label{sec:noise}
The crossover in Table~\ref{tab:contention} has a specific, documented mechanism, not a diffuse ``overhead'' explanation. The Linux kernel scheduler documentation states that once any single CPU's utilization crosses the overutilization tipping point, 80\% of capacity by default, the \texttt{overutilized} flag is raised for the entire root domain, EAS is disabled, and the generic, fairness-driven load balancer is re-enabled for both wake-up placement and periodic balancing \cite{linuxeas}. The threshold is per-CPU but the consequence is domain-wide: one saturated core withdraws capacity-aware placement from all twenty. EAS is thus available exactly when it is least needed and withdrawn exactly when placement decisions matter most.

This is what the Performance cluster experiences under our noise conditions. With 10 background threads, or with 8--20 threads from co-running tenants, the high-capacity cluster crosses into overutilization, EAS's energy- and capacity-aware placement deactivates, and the fallback balancer, which has no notion of which thread lies on the DNN's critical path, resumes control. That balancer is free to migrate a pending Layer 8 (deconvolution) task, 54.8\% of per-frame cost by the isolated profile of Section~\ref{sec:layerprofile}, onto an Efficient core in the name of queue-length fairness, incurring a straggler penalty of up to 1.83--1.90$\times$ on the critical path.

Static pinning admits no such migration path. Heavy stages stay on Performance cores and instead share CPU time with the noise through ordinary context switching, which raises average latency: static pinning is 23.3\% slower under heavy noise than on an idle system. But it raises it smoothly. The failure mode it eliminates is not contention, which no policy can eliminate, but contention-triggered misplacement. This also explains the dispersion result: the fallback balancer's migration decisions vary from run to run, so CFS/EAS's standard deviation nearly quadruples under load, from 12.4 to 46.7~ms, while static pinning's does not move (19.7 to 13.0~ms).

Two boundaries on this interpretation deserve stating. We infer the migration mechanism from the documented kernel policy and the shape of our measurements; we did not instrument \texttt{sched\_switch}/\texttt{sched\_migrate\_task} tracepoints to observe stage-to-Efficient-core migrations directly, which would convert this from a well-supported inference into a demonstration. And our background threads are themselves unpinned; a variant pinned specifically to the Performance cluster would represent a harsher worst case that we did not test.

\subsection{When Static Pinning Is the Wrong Choice}
\label{sec:whenwrong}
The results do not support a general recommendation to hard-pin. They support a conditional one. On a machine that stays idle for the duration of a run (a dedicated inference appliance, a benchmark rig, a batch job with the node to itself) CFS/EAS was measurably better in our experiments, and the 8.2\% it gains is real. The case for pinning rests on the deployment being contended, and on latency predictability being worth more than best-case throughput.

The magnitude of the effect should also be read against this platform's unusually narrow cluster gap. At 1.83$\times$, a misplaced critical-path stage on the GX10 is a moderate penalty. On a Cortex-A76/A55-class platform with a 3--4$\times$ gap, the same misplacement is far more expensive, so we would expect the crossover to appear at lower contention and the margins to be wider. This is an extrapolation from the mechanism, not a measurement, and platforms with a true ultra-efficiency tier may also trip the overutilization threshold at different points. Confirming the crossover on a classic big.LITTLE SoC is the most valuable single follow-up experiment this work suggests.

Frequency is also a poor proxy for capacity, and this constrains any policy that needs a capacity ratio, including a correctly-set gating threshold. The two clusters' maximum frequencies, read per core from \texttt{cpufreq}, differ by $3.900/2.808 = 1.39\times$, but their measured throughput on identical single-threaded work differs by 1.83--1.90$\times$ across two sessions (Section~\ref{sec:idle}), a 32--37\% discrepancy attributable to microarchitectural width, cache and sustained-clock behaviour, not to nominal clock. This matters concretely: SyncPilot's own cluster detection reads \texttt{cpufreq/cpuinfo\_max\_freq}, which is sufficient to \emph{rank} the clusters, all our pinning needs, but would badly misestimate the capacity ratio if a policy tried to size a load partition from it. The implication points somewhere useful for future work: runtime calibration has value on this platform, but not for the purpose we originally built it. Measuring per-stage cost proved unstable and counterproductive (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation}, \ref{sec:layerprofile}); measuring per-\emph{cluster} capacity is a single scalar, is cheap to obtain from a short identical microbenchmark on one core of each class, and, unlike the per-stage table, was reproducible to within 0.5\% across our repeats. It is the one quantity frequency cannot supply.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations should be stated explicitly. First, jitter is characterized at run granularity, not frame granularity: our instrumentation records one wall-clock time per 150-frame run, so we report run-to-run standard deviation and worst-of-seven runs, not per-frame p95/p99 tail latency. This is the largest remaining gap, because latency predictability is the paper's central claim. Second, we did not directly observe thread migrations; the EAS-disable mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:noise} is inferred from kernel documentation plus the shape of our results, not from scheduler tracepoints. Third, the results come from a single platform with an atypically narrow Performance/Efficient capacity ratio (1.83--1.90$\times$), and generalization to conventional big.LITTLE parts is untested. Fourth, the $0.75\times$ mean-cost threshold used by the ablated cost-gating policy has not been swept, so we can report that this policy loses at that setting but not that no threshold would win; our instability finding does, however, apply to any fixed threshold on the same table. Fifth, the failed-scan and capacity-blind-partition mechanisms we propose for the gating penalty are derived from the dispatch code and the measured capacity ratio, and are not instrumented at runtime; we did not count rejected scans or measure per-cluster idle time. Sixth, the counter measurements of Section~\ref{sec:perfcounter} are single runs per configuration, not averages, and PMU sampling coverage falls to 93.2\% at twenty workers. Seventh, our noise threads and tenants are unpinned; a Performance-cluster-pinned adversary is untested. Eighth, all energy claims implied by ``Efficient core'' terminology are motivational, not measured: we collected no power telemetry, and quantifying the energy cost of hard-pinning versus CFS/EAS is left entirely to future work, which matters here because EAS is an energy-aware policy that we have evaluated only on latency. Ninth, we did not benchmark frame-level parallelism (a \texttt{parallel for} over independent frames) as an alternative to stage pipelining; the pipeline is motivated by bounded in-flight memory and applicability to streaming inputs, but we cannot claim it is faster. Finally, no artifact or code package accompanies this submission; we intend to release one in a revision.

\section{Conclusion}
We asked whether an application should override Linux CFS/EAS with explicit static core pinning for pipeline-parallel DNN inference on an asymmetric multicore, and we answered it across a contention sweep instead of at the idle operating point where the question is usually settled. On an idle ASUS GX10, the OS wins: unpinned CFS/EAS completes our 8-stage FSRCNN pipeline in 1050~ms against static pinning's 1136~ms, 8.2\% faster. At every load condition we tested, the ordering inverts. Static pinning leads by 3.1\% at 4 background threads, 19.8\% at 10, and 8.2--12.0\% with 2--5 co-running inference tenants, all at $p<10^{-4}$, and it degrades far more predictably, with run-to-run standard deviation of 13.0~ms against 46.7~ms and worst-case run latency growing 23.1\% from idle against 71.4\%. The mechanism is the kernel's documented disabling of EAS once the root domain is overutilized, after which a fairness-driven balancer with no model of the critical path may migrate the deconvolution stage, 54.8\% of per-frame cost, onto a core 1.83--1.90$\times$ slower.

A second result bounds the claim. Layering cost-based stage routing on top of pinning, using a one-time per-stage cost calibration, is slower than plain pinning at all seven contention levels by 3.6--8.3\%. The reason matters. Measured in isolation the per-stage cost model is near-deterministic, with 1\% run-to-run spread across ten runs. But the table the framework actually builds is calibrated inside the running pipeline, and its spread grows monotonically with concurrency to 2.52$\times$ at twenty workers, flipping the heavy/light partition outright in 4 of 60 runs. Even when it does not flip, the threshold hands 74--78\% of measured cost to the Performance cluster against the 65.5\% a capacity-proportional split would justify. A cost threshold fixes the load partition from the workload alone; emergent pulling under plain pinning lets it settle at the hardware's actual capacity ratio instead. The win belongs to topology-aware static placement, which needs no runtime measurement at all. Correctness is unaffected throughout: 27 of the 28 distinct configurations reported produced bit-exact output, the exception being a naive OpenMP control that corrupts 40.1\% of output bytes.

In practice, scheduler comparisons on quiesced machines answer a question that deployed edge devices do not ask. It also suggests where runtime calibration does belong. Nominal frequency understates this platform's cluster capacity ratio by 32--37\% (1.39$\times$ nominal against 1.83--1.90$\times$ measured), so any policy that needs a capacity ratio cannot read one from \texttt{cpufreq}; measuring one scalar per cluster is cheap, stable, and exactly what per-stage cost measurement failed to be. Future work should confirm the crossover on a conventional big.LITTLE platform where the cluster capacity gap is wider, instrument scheduler tracepoints to observe the migrations we currently infer, and measure the energy side of the trade-off, since the policy we recommend against is by design the energy-aware one.
